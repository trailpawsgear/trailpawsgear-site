Most of the GPS trackers we cover on this site are built around one problem: a dog running flat-out, off-leash, miles from a trailhead. A senior dog's risk looks nothing like that. The dogs who go missing later in life usually aren't sprinting after a deer — they slip out an open gate, get disoriented a block from home, or wander off mid-walk because an aging brain loses the thread of where it's going. Canine cognitive dysfunction, the dog version of dementia, is common enough in dogs past about ten that vets consider it a normal thing to screen for, and disorientation is one of its earliest signs.

That changes what actually matters in a tracker. Range and rugged waterproofing matter less than a tight, reliable geofence that alerts you the moment a confused dog steps past the yard line; a collar light enough not to bother arthritic necks; a battery you can realistically remember to charge; and, increasingly, built-in health or activity monitoring that can flag a slowdown before you'd otherwise notice one. We compared four trackers that approach the job differently — two GPS collars with health features, one ultra-light cellular tag built for small and fragile bodies, and one zero-subscription Bluetooth tag for dogs who mostly stay close to home.

A note on Whistle

If you've seen Whistle Go Explore recommended elsewhere, know that the entire Whistle product line was discontinued in 2025 after Tractive acquired the brand — the devices no longer activate and existing subscriptions were shut down. Don't buy one secondhand. Tractive's own GPS DOG 6, included above, is the closest like-for-like replacement.

What actually matters for an older dog

A tight, reliable geofence beats raw tracking range for this use case, because the goal is catching a wandering dog within yards of home, not following one for miles. Battery life matters more than it does for an active trail dog too — a senior dog is less likely to be out on a long hike where you'd notice a dead collar right away, so a tracker that dies quietly on a kitchen counter is a real risk. And where it's offered, activity or sleep monitoring is worth the extra membership cost: a gradual drop in movement or a change in sleep pattern is often the first sign of arthritis, cognitive decline, or another condition worth a vet visit, well before it's obvious on a daily walk.

The short version

For most older dogs, the Fi Series 3 is the collar we'd put on first — the activity and sleep tracking gives you an early warning system beyond just location, and the three-month battery means fewer chances to forget a charge. If the ongoing cost is the deciding factor, the Tractive GPS DOG 6 gets most of the same job done for less, with genuinely useful Power Saving Zones for a dog that rarely leaves the property. A small, frail, or toy-breed senior is better served by the Jiobit's minimal weight than by a bulkier collar. And if your dog is reliably contained by a fence and you just want a cheap backup in case it slips out, the Apple AirTag covers that one job without a subscription — but it's not a substitute for real GPS if your dog is prone to wandering any real distance.
